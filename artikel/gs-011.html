The Dissolving Leviathan: How Digitalization Abolishes the Conditions That Make States Possible

There is a story we tell ourselves about technological change, and it goes like this: things look alarming at first, institutions struggle, then they adapt, and life goes on. Steam didn't destroy society. Electricity didn't destroy society. The internet, we are quietly assured, will not destroy society either. States have always found a way.

I want to argue that this story, while historically defensible up to a point, fails precisely at the moment it matters most. The reason is not that digitalization is more dramatic than prior transitions, though it is. The reason is that it is categorically different in a way that the adaptation narrative cannot accommodate. Every previous technological revolution changed what was inside the institutional containers we call states. Digitalization is dissolving the containers themselves.

The Comforting Continuity That Isn't

The optimistic default position on digitalization and governance treats our current situation as one more chapter in a long series of institutional adjustments. And it is easy to see why. Every technological disruption since the industrial revolution generated panicked predictions of collapse, and every time, states adapted. They built new regulatory frameworks. They extended their tax bases. They rewrote the rules of commerce and war.

But there is a precise reason why this historical analogy misleads us now, and I think clarity about that precision matters enormously. Prior transitions — steam, electricity, telecommunications — altered the contents of institutional containers while leaving the containers themselves structurally intact. The territorial state, with its capacity to tax visible economic activity, project force across a defined geography, and monopolize organized violence, remained the natural unit of political organization throughout. What changed was what happened inside that unit.

Digitalization is different in kind. It does not change what happens inside the territorial state. It erodes the three physical preconditions — visible taxable surplus, geographic containment, and monopoly on violence — without which the territorial state cannot function as a coherent institutional form. This is not a problem of adjustment. It is a problem of substrate.

What Friction Was Actually Doing

Before the age of digital machines, every algorithm ran on human wetware. Bureaucracies, legal codes, court systems, social norms — these were all information-processing systems implemented in biological hardware, with all the slowness, opacity, and cognitive limitation that implies.

We tend to regard that friction as a pure cost, an inefficiency to be engineered away. But I think this misses something important. The friction in pre-digital institutional systems was not incidental to their political function. It was constitutive of it. A law that takes months to interpret and years to litigate is a law that humans can contest, delay, and reform. Bureaucratic slowness created the temporal space in which democratic correction became possible. The cognitive limits on coordination at scale meant that large-scale organization required geographic proximity, which meant it remained visible, locatable, and therefore subject to oversight and control.

Silicon algorithms remove those limits entirely. They execute coordination at zero marginal cost and infinite scale, with no requirement for geographic concentration and no natural friction that might create space for institutional response. This is not simply faster wetware. It is a phase transition out of the logic that made wetware-based institutions politically tractable, into something for which we have, as yet, no adequate political vocabulary.

Platforms Are Not State-Like. They Are States.

In the nineteenth century, the German jurist Georg Jelinek formulated what became the classical definition of statehood: a state is an entity with territory, population, and power. This triad was formulated for geographic entities, and we have applied it habitually to geographic entities ever since. But if I apply it rigorously rather than habitually, something unexpected emerges.

Google, Apple, and BYD satisfy Jelinek's triad as well as or better than many recognized states. Each commands a bounded infrastructure that functions as territory. Each governs a population — not through citizenship, but through account deletion and behavioral banning, which is internal power by any functional definition. And each possesses the capacity to disable the smartphones or autonomous vehicles of an entire nation at a decision point, which is external power of a kind that many recognized states cannot match.

The standard response is to call these entities "state-like" and move on, as if the qualifier resolved the problem. But the qualifier does not resolve anything. These platforms do not merely resemble states in some loose metaphorical sense. They satisfy the formal definition. What they constitute is a new genus of non-territorial state — one whose fundamental operating principle, simultaneous multi-membership, directly contradicts the exclusivity axiom on which Westphalian sovereignty was built. The international system of sovereign states assumes that each person belongs to exactly one sovereign entity. The platform model assumes the opposite.

Infrastructure, Exit, and the Asymmetry Democratic Theory Cannot Process

There is a useful conceptual frame for understanding why platforms and states belong in the same structural category, and it comes from thinking carefully about what infrastructure actually is. Infrastructure is precisely what markets need but cannot produce themselves — the preconditions for exchange rather than the products of exchange. Roads, legal systems, communication standards, App Stores — all of these are infrastructure in the relevant sense.

This functional definition places territorial states and digital platforms in the same structural category. Both provide the foundational conditions within which economic and social life becomes possible. What differs, and what differs in a way that democratic theory is completely unprepared to process, is the membership model.

I am a citizen of one country, and I cannot easily change that. The exit costs are enormous — legal, financial, linguistic, familial, psychological. The state I was born into exercises a kind of gravitational hold that is enforced not only by law but by the entire weight of my social existence. I am simultaneously a user of a dozen platform ecosystems, and I can leave any of them before lunch, at zero cost, with no meaningful consequence. The asymmetry between these two exit structures is not a minor technical detail. It represents a fundamental difference in the relationship between governed entities and governing institutions — and democratic theory, built entirely around the first model, has no vocabulary to process the second.

The Grain That Is No Longer There

The historian James Scott showed that states did not emerge arbitrarily from the landscape of human social organization. They crystallized historically around agricultural surplus, and specifically around grain, because grain has properties that make it administratively tractable. It is sedentary — it stays where you plant it. It is countable — you can measure it. And it is confiscatable — the tax collector can see it, store it, and seize it without the owner's cooperation.

This insight illuminates something about the current moment that is often framed too narrowly as a problem of tax evasion or regulatory arbitrage. Remote labor paid in cryptocurrency for intangible services hosted in cloud infrastructure replicates, at a civilizational scale, the condition of the pre-state mobile forager whose harvest is invisible and untraceable. The state's tax collector cannot confiscate what the state's tax collector cannot see, and the material properties of digital value production mean that vast swathes of modern economic activity simply do not appear on the administrative horizon in the way that grain, then manufacturing output, then registered financial transactions once did.

This is not primarily a story about people deliberately hiding money, though that happens too. It is a story about structural invisibility at the level of ontology. Digitalized value does not hide from the state. It simply lacks the material properties that made taxation possible in the first place. The fiscal foundation of the territorial state is not being undermined by clever lawyers and offshore accounts. It is being unmade at a more fundamental level than any legal reform can reach.

When the Protection Racket Loses Its Market

Charles Tilly's account of state formation is bracingly unsentimental: states are protection rackets that achieved monopoly scale. The process of centralized standing-army warfare both required and reproduced the institutional apparatus that made interstate violence manageable — professional diplomacy, the Geneva and Hague conventions, clearly defined combatant status, the fundamental distinction between state and non-state actors that allowed the whole system to function as a system rather than as chaos.

The shift to decentralized cyber and hybrid warfare dissolves every one of those de-escalation mechanisms simultaneously. A terrorist cell is now structurally indistinguishable from a startup. Both require a small team, digital infrastructure, modest capital, and no geographic base. The market entry barriers for destruction have collapsed alongside those for creation, and the institutional apparatus built to manage escalation presupposes distinctions — between war and crime, between combatant and civilian, between state and non-state actor — that no longer hold technically or organizationally.

This leaves a definitional vacuum at precisely the point where miscalculation is most catastrophic. It is not simply that we need better cybersecurity law or updated international conventions, though both would help at the margins. It is that the entire framework of managed interstate violence rests on structural conditions that are disappearing.

Coase at the Limit

Ronald Coase's elegant explanation of why firms exist — that they are the organizational response to transaction costs that exceed what markets can handle efficiently — was extended by Oliver Williamson into a broader theory of institutions. Hierarchy, bureaucracy, and ultimately the state itself are institutional solutions to coordination problems generated by friction. They exist because coordination in their absence costs more.

Digitalization attacks this logic from the inside. It first collapses internal transaction costs within firms, enabling smaller and more fluid organizational forms. Then it drives all-actor transaction costs progressively toward zero, removing the economic rationale for large corporations and bureaucratic states alike. If the foundational problem that large institutions solve is coordination under friction, and the friction is disappearing, then large institutions are not being made more efficient. They are being made redundant.

What is required, following this logic to its conclusion, is not incremental reform of existing institutions. It is entirely new institutional forms adequate to a world in which the foundational friction has disappeared. The challenge is not to make the state more efficient. It is to understand what, if anything, should replace the functions the state performed in an institutional environment that no longer exists.

The Boundary That Is Not a Gradient

The cumulative weight of these arguments — the dissolution of taxable surplus, the emergence of non-territorial states that satisfy classical statehood criteria, the collapse of geographic power monopoly, the disappearance of transaction-cost rationale for large institutions — does not add up to a picture of smooth institutional evolution. It adds up to something qualitatively different: a non-continuous topological boundary between two fundamentally different kinds of world.

On one side of that boundary, the territorial state is the natural unit of political organization. It is the entity around which international law, democratic theory, fiscal systems, and security arrangements are coherently organized. On the other side, the territorial state is an evolutionary relic, maintained by institutional inertia and the deep sentimental attachment of populations who have no other political vocabulary, but structurally unequal to the entities and processes that actually govern the conditions of human life.

We are at that boundary now. The central intellectual and political task of this moment is to recognize it honestly — to resist the temptation to paper over it with optimistic continuity narratives dressed up as pragmatism — and to begin the genuinely difficult work of constructing institutional forms adequate to the world that lies on the other side.

The territorial state is not being reformed by digitalization. It is being stranded. And only by naming that honestly can we begin to build what comes next.
