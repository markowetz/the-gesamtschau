Obsolete by Design: How Digitalization Dismantles the Institutions That Cognition Built

Property, labor, and the corporation are not timeless pillars of civilization. They are emergency workarounds for a brain too small to govern complex societies — and the digital revolution is quietly making those workarounds redundant.

The Number That Built Civilization

I want to start with a number: 150.

That is roughly the ceiling of stable social relationships the human brain can maintain simultaneously — what the anthropologist Robin Dunbar derived from primate neocortex ratios and then confirmed across military units, village records, and Christmas card lists. It sounds like a quirky data point, the kind of thing you mention at dinner parties and promptly forget. But I think it is something far more consequential. It is the founding constraint of civilization — the invisible wall against which every major institutional invention humanity has ever produced was constructed.

Once communities scaled beyond 150 people, the informal trust networks that governed sharing, reciprocity, and resource allocation simply collapsed under their own weight. There was no longer a shared social memory capable of tracking who owed what to whom, who had defected from a collective agreement, or who was overusing a common resource. The cognitive ledger was full. And so what followed was not a moral awakening or a spontaneous political revolution. It was engineering. Institutional design, properly understood, is epistemic engineering: the art of building systems that allow minds too small for complexity to coordinate within it anyway.

Property rights, employment contracts, the limited liability corporation — each of these is a downstream response to that cognitive ceiling. Each is an algorithm for solving a coordination problem that the brain, left to its own devices, cannot solve. And each is now being quietly dismantled by the digital revolution, not because anyone planned it that way, but because digitalization is systematically eliminating the conditions that made those algorithms necessary in the first place.

Property Was Never What We Thought It Was

Consider the tragedy of the commons. When a shared pasture is used by a community of 200 farmers, no single person has the cognitive bandwidth to monitor who is overgrazing. The information is too dispersed, the relationships too numerous, the violations too incremental. The monitoring problem is cognitively intractable at that scale.

The classical solution — exclusive private ownership — does not resolve this by virtue of being just or natural or philosophically correct. It resolves it by reducing the gatekeeper problem to a single accountable agent per object. One owner, one access lock, one point of accountability. The distributed surveillance problem is compressed into a single permission node, and suddenly the cognitive load becomes manageable.

Property, in other words, is a coordination hack. A clever and historically enormously productive hack, but a hack nonetheless.

History then dressed this hack in the language of natural rights, moral desert, and individual liberty. That rhetorical clothing was extraordinarily effective. It made a contingent engineering solution feel like metaphysical bedrock, the kind of thing you could not question without seeming to attack civilization itself. But strip the philosophy away and what remains is an algorithm — one owner, one access lock, one point of accountability. Useful, certainly. Not sacred.

The Firm Was Always a Cost Reduction Strategy

In 1937, the economist Ronald Coase asked what seemed like a naïve question: if markets are so efficient, why do firms exist? Why don't companies simply outsource every task to independent contractors negotiating per-assignment in an open market? His answer was that repeated per-task negotiation is prohibitively expensive. The transaction costs — the time, effort, and uncertainty involved in writing and enforcing countless small contracts — make it cheaper to internalize those transactions within a single organizational structure. The firm exists to avoid paying those costs constantly.

The 19th-century labor contract does exactly the same thing at the individual level. Rather than renegotiating every morning what a worker will do, for how long, and for what compensation, the employment relationship bundles all of those micro-agreements into one long-running deal. It is not a noble covenant between capital and labor. It is a cost reduction strategy wearing institutional clothing.

Neither the employment relationship nor the corporation, properly understood, has a principled justification beyond this. That observation might seem cynical, but I mean it as clarifying. It means that both institutions are fundamentally contingent — contingent on the level of transaction costs that prevails in the economy at any given moment. Reduce those costs dramatically and you remove the foundation beneath both structures at once.

The Category Error at the Heart of Intellectual Property

The property algorithm was designed for a specific class of objects: things that cannot be in two places at once. A field, a hammer, a cow — what economists call rivalrous goods. The moment I am using them, you cannot. That mutual exclusivity is exactly what gives exclusive ownership its logic. Assign one person the right to control access and you have solved the monitoring problem without requiring anyone to track anything beyond that single assignment.

The moment someone extended this logic to ideas, however, they committed a category error so fundamental that we are still living with its consequences. An idea used by one person is not thereby unavailable to another. The scarcity that justifies exclusive ownership simply does not exist in the domain of information. Intellectual property rights do not reflect a natural feature of ideas; they impose an artificial scarcity on goods that are, by their nature, non-rivalrous.

What digital platforms like YouTube already demonstrate — imperfectly, inconsistently, but unmistakably — is the superior alternative. Rather than granting creators a monopoly lock that must be enforced through litigation and digital rights management, the platform replaces prior-permission gatekeeping with continuous usage tracking and automated micro-payments. Copyright dissolves into a live revenue stream. The creator is compensated not by locking the work away but by measuring how the work flows through the world. The access-denial algorithm is replaced by an access-tracking algorithm, and the original category error is corrected without destroying the economic incentive to create.

Why Socialism Proves My Point Rather Than Refuting It

At this stage, an objection usually arrives wearing red: does not socialism challenge everything I have argued by proposing the outright abolition of private property? The answer is that socialism, examined closely, confirms rather than refutes my thesis.

Look at what socialism actually does. It does not eliminate the monopoly-access lock. It transfers it from individual owners to the state. The lock remains. The algorithm persists. The coordination mechanism survives intact. Only the identity of the entity holding the key changes.

This is the strongest possible evidence for my argument. Every society in history that has attempted large-scale coordination has preserved some access-control mechanism, because without it the monitoring problem that Dunbar's number generates simply has no solution at the relevant scale. When socialist planners discovered this — often at considerable human cost — they did not abolish ownership; they nationalized it. They kept the algorithm and swapped the owner. The socialist experiment did not falsify the thesis that property is a coordination algorithm. It demonstrated that the algorithm is irreplaceable, that the question is never whether to have it but only who holds it and how.

What Digital Technology Is Actually Doing to These Structures

Digital platforms are doing something structurally unprecedented in economic history: they are driving negotiation costs, monitoring costs, and enforcement costs toward zero simultaneously. When those costs approach zero, the original justifications for property transfers, employment contracts, and vertically integrated corporations do not merely weaken. They dissolve together, all at once, because they were all responses to the same underlying problem.

Here is what becomes possible when that happens. Persistent digital channels can be maintained between producers and the products they create, carrying nano-scale profit shares in real time along entire value chains. A musician receives a continuous micro-payment as their recording generates value downstream. A software developer gets a live revenue share as their library is used by applications they will never see. A machinist whose labor contributed to a component three steps upstream in a supply chain receives an automated payment as the final product sells. The connection between producer and product, once severed the moment the wage was paid, is restored by a persistent data channel and an automated payment protocol.

This resolves what Marx identified as alienation — the severing of workers from the products of their labor — with considerably more precision than Marx's proposed solution. It does not require revolution. It does not require seizing the means of production or abolishing wage labor by decree. It requires engineering: the right data architecture and the right payment infrastructure. The producer is reattached to the product not by changing who owns the factory but by changing what information flows through the economy in real time.

What the Digital Revolution Cannot Dissolve

I want to be careful not to overstate the thesis. The collapse of transaction costs does not predict the end of all large institutions. It predicts something more precise: the end of institutions whose scale was justified by coordination friction alone.

Where physical economies of scale remain irreducible — in heavy manufacturing, in infrastructure networks, in energy grids — organizational consolidation persists, and it persists for good reason. A steel mill does not become cheaper to operate by disaggregating it into a network of micro-contractors settling accounts on a blockchain. The physics of the furnace is indifferent to your platform architecture. The enormous capital requirements of industrial production, the thermodynamic realities of large-scale energy generation, the logistical dependencies of physical infrastructure — none of these are transaction costs. None of them disappear when negotiation becomes frictionless.

This distinction draws a principled boundary that the debate about digitalization often lacks. The institutions that digitalization renders obsolete are those whose scale existed solely to manage coordination friction. The institutions that digitalization merely reforms are those whose scale reflects physical and thermodynamic realities that no software layer can dissolve. Confusing the two categories produces both utopian overreach and reactionary denial in roughly equal measure.

Outgrowing the Workarounds

If property, labor, and the corporation were always algorithms rather than axioms — contingent engineering solutions to the problem of coordinating minds too small for complexity — then digitalization is not attacking civilization's foundations. It is finally equipping us with the cognitive prosthetics to outgrow the workarounds we mistook for bedrock.

The workarounds served us well. For millennia, there was simply no better answer to the monitoring problems that Dunbar's number generates at scale. The property algorithm compressed distributed surveillance into a single permission node. The employment contract compressed endless micro-negotiations into one long-running deal. The corporation compressed the transaction costs of market exchange into a single organizational boundary. These were genuine achievements of institutional design, and the civilizations they made possible are real.

But the fact that a workaround was necessary does not make it permanent. The fact that we needed it for so long does not make it natural. And the fact that we have now built the infrastructure to replace it with something better does not make that replacement a catastrophe — unless we are too intellectually attached to the solutions of the past to recognize better ones when they arrive.

The question is not whether these institutions will change. They are already changing, in ways that institutional theory has struggled to keep pace with. The question is whether we are honest enough to stop mourning the loss of workarounds and clear-eyed enough to start designing the systems that should replace them.
