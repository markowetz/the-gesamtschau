States Are Institutions Built on Wetware — Digitalization Makes Them Obsolete

Digitalization is not a technical upgrade. What it actually does is dissolve the institutional foundations that states, armies, and tax collectors have rested on for two centuries. Corporations, standing armies, and revenue bureaucracies were all solutions to the same underlying problem: how do you coordinate large numbers of people across space and time when the only substrate available is the human brain, the human hand, and ink on paper?

The Algorithm Beneath the Institution

A single assumption change generates a genuinely new algorithmic problem. A binary tree works in main memory; swap in a block-based hard disk and the cost model shifts from RAM access to disk I/O, forcing an entirely different structure — the B-tree. Distribute across machines and network I/O becomes the dominant cost.

Swap silicon for wetware — the human brain, eyes, and fingers holding a pen — and you enter an entirely different algorithmic class. That shift defines what I call social algorithms: algorithmics running on wetware rather than on any machine. Every institution humanity built before digitalization ran on this substrate: laws copied by scribes, taxes assessed by inspectors, armies commanded by shouting officers.

Why Corporations Exist — and Why They Will Not

Firms exist because hardwired communication slashes internal transaction costs. Ronald Coase answered this question ninety years ago: wire people together inside a single organization and coordination costs drop dramatically. The internal overhead is real, but the competitive advantage over uncoordinated rivals is greater still.

Full digitalization collapses those costs for everyone — including those outside the firm. Amazon is the first step: internal coordination costs drop toward zero, and the firm wins. The second step is more radical — when transaction costs sink toward zero for everyone outside the firm too, the corporation's structural advantage evaporates entirely.

Platforms Are States

Infrastructure is what markets need but cannot produce themselves. A legal system is the clearest example: corporations need courts to resolve disputes, yet no company can sell judicial authority in competition with another. By this definition, Apple's App Store and Google's ecosystem qualify without ambiguity. Any developer can write an app and compete with every other; no single developer can build the integrated hardware, platform, and market reach that makes the store itself possible. The store is not one competitor among others — it is the precondition for competition.

Georg Jellinek identified three foundations of any territorial national state: territory, a population, and two distinct forms of power. Internal power — police — enforces order within the borders; external power — the army — defends against other states. Google meets all three criteria. Its infrastructure spans Search, Gmail, Maps, and a globally redundant cloud network. Android runs on roughly two billion devices. Internal power is blunt: one decision deletes your account and locks you out permanently. External power is subtler but just as real — Google controls approximately 80 percent of German smartphones. Brick those devices overnight and you inflict the precise telecommunications catastrophe Cold War strategists once feared from Soviet weapons.

Chinese electric vehicles with covert channels hand Beijing the same reach over Europe. If thirty percent of German private vehicles run on Chinese software, the threat is not a cyberattack. It is a siege. The decisive structural difference between states and platforms is exclusive membership: a citizen holds one passport, a user holds hundreds of accounts simultaneously, and if one platform turns hostile, ninety-nine alternatives remain. That multiplicity creates a power mechanism with no name yet in political theory — and nameless forces are the most dangerous ones.

Wars, States, and the Machinery Between Them

Charles Tilly is almost never read at German universities, and that absence is itself revealing. Like James Scott and David Graeber, Tilly studies worlds radically different from our own. He never takes the modern state, taxation, or organized violence as given, because his entire archive shows they could have been otherwise.

His most famous claim: states are extortion rackets. An organization arrives at your door, declares you under threat, offers protection, and demands payment — and if you refuse, something bad happens to you. His second core claim follows immediately: wars make states and states make wars. To wage war a ruler needs revenue; collecting revenue requires a bureaucracy; that bureaucracy is the state.

The professional standing army is only two hundred years old. Frederick the Great created a permanent force funded by a permanent tax bureaucracy, staffed by interchangeable officers loyal to the state rather than to a local lord. Every major war since then produced a new de-escalation mechanism: the Congress of Vienna institutionalized diplomacy, guaranteed safe passage for ambassadors, and made killing a diplomat simply off the table. Those protections do not map onto new power actors. When Huawei's CFO flew to Canada, she functioned as a de facto state-level emissary — yet Canada arrested her on an American warrant, something it could never have done to China's ambassador.

The Geneva Convention's definitions no longer map onto current conflict. A hacker in a basement causes damage on another continent without crossing a border. A private satellite network guides missile targeting without deploying a single soldier. Yet no existing legal category captures any of this as warfare. Without a definition there is no threshold, and without a threshold there is no de-escalation mechanism to trigger. The cost of inflicting serious damage has meanwhile collapsed to a laptop and four people: a DJI drone costs 500 euros; ten of them over a full stadium kill ten and trample 250 more — total outlay 5,000 euros. The standing armies built since Napoleon to match nation-state-scale destruction are solving the wrong problem.

Centralized wars end the moment a root node surrenders — Germany in 1945 proves this. Decentralized networks have no such node. The emerging topology of conflict is networked rather than geographic: it runs through supply chains, satellite links, and corporate platforms that sprawl far beyond any belligerent state. No framework exists to draw a ceasefire line across those structures.

The Legibility of the State — and Its Collapse

States invented surnames to make populations legible and taxable. James Scott's Seeing Like a State traces exactly this move: before state intervention, a village managed its own Peters by deed — Peter-who-rode-the-cow, Peter-who-slew-the-dragon. The state assigned surnames, and suddenly a population became a ledger.

Scott's Against the Grain locates the deeper mechanism: states only emerge where food can be seen, stored, and seized. Grain ripens all at once, sits in a granary, and the tax collector simply takes his share. No storable, seizable crop means no tax base, no army, no bureaucracy, no state. Digital labor paid in Bitcoin is invisible and unseizable. A developer in Marburg does AI work for an Indonesian company, collects Bitcoin, and tells the tax collector she owns nothing — because on paper she does. The granary is empty.

What Comes After

States are not natural features of the landscape. They are institutional solutions to the specific coordination problems that wetware-based social algorithms generate — and digitalization removes those problems one by one. The choice is not whether these institutions will be disrupted; they already are. The only question that remains is whether the successor structures carry forward the protections those old institutions, at their best, actually provided.
